# Resting and Degassing Coffee

## Overview
Why roasted coffee needs to rest before brewing, the CO2 chemistry behind it, and how long to wait depending on roast level and brew method. Source: James Hoffmann, "A Beginner's Guide to Resting Coffee" (YouTube, 2021-12-29).

## Key Points

### CO2 chemistry & roast level
- Roasting drives browning reactions that produce CO2 as a byproduct — often cited as ~10 liters of CO2 per kilo of coffee, though most escapes during roasting itself.
- Darker roasts produce more CO2 but the bean is more porous, so it escapes faster; lighter roasts produce less CO2 but the denser bean traps it longer, so it degasses slower.
- Higher-grown (denser) beans degas more slowly regardless of roast level.

### Brewing effects
- Water hitting grounds during brewing liberates trapped CO2 — visible as the "bloom" in filter brewing (fresher/darker coffee = bigger bloom).
- In espresso, CO2 escaping during extraction disrupts flow and makes it harder to extract properly — resting matters more for espresso than filter.

### Storage & containers
- **Quenching**: some roasters mist water onto beans post-roast for fast evaporative cooling (needed for large batches to avoid the bed continuing to roast/catching fire). More quenching = faster outgassing but also faster staling. Distinct from adding water to increase weight, which is a known scam that ruins flavor and shelf life.
- Storage temperature affects degassing rate: warmer = faster degas but also faster staling/oxidation. ~20°C is a reasonable ceiling; freezing preserves freshness while slowing degassing (frozen coffee can still pull good crema and taste fresh).
- One-way bag valves let CO2 out without letting fresh oxygen in. They do **not** meaningfully "gas-flush" the bag — CO2 doesn't selectively displace air, it takes air out at a similar ratio. Valves are essential on ground coffee (ungrounded whole bean bags can survive without one; ground coffee bags will burst from pressure without a valve).
- **Vacuum-sealed containers/jars**: pulling a vacuum on fresh coffee looks like it "fails" over the next few days as the beans continue degassing and refill the vacuum space with CO2 — this is normal beans off-gassing, not a faulty seal or oxygen re-entering. After the first vacuum re-equalizes (often 3-4 days), it typically holds thereafter. Vacuum storage doesn't stop degassing, it just prevents fresh oxygen from causing oxidation/staling while the CO2 escapes.

### Rest time recommendations
Hoffmann's rules of thumb, adjust for storage temp — extend if cold, shorten if warm:
- **Filter**: dark roast ~2 days; light/medium ~4-5 days; very light/dense beans up to 10+ days. Even next-day is drinkable, just not optimal.
- **Espresso** (much more sensitive to CO2): light roast ~10 days; medium ~8-10 days; dark roast ~2 days (dark roasts are more about staling risk than CO2, so freshness matters more than long rest).

### Practical takeaways
- Under-rested coffee tastes sour/under-extracted, sometimes with a "carbonic acid tang" like flat sparkling water; occasionally a faint smoky note if extremely fresh.
- Resting is about ease of brewing/consistency, not transforming bad coffee into great coffee — don't over-index on hitting a "perfect" rest window.
